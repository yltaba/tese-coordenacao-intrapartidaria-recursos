Length of candidate lists and political
careers: Quasi-experimental evidence from
Brazil
Henrique Augusto Campos Fernandez Hott
University of São Paulo, São Paulo, Brazil
Naercio Menezes-Filho
Insper and University of São Paulo, São Paulo, Brazil
Abstract
This paper provides causal estimates of the effects of increases in the size of candidate lists on the political careers of
newcomers to the political arena. We explore an exogenous discontinuity in district magnitude that allows parties to
expand their candidate lists in the Brazilian open-list local elections and ﬁnd that the additional candidacies are allocated to
weaker candidates, mainly newcomers, who end up occupying a signiﬁcant share of the additional seats. We also ﬁnd that
the electoral experience provided by the additional candidacies increases the number of newcomers elected in their second
attempt.
Keywords
candidate lists, open list proportional representation, political entry, party selection, regression discontinuity
Introduction
A growing literature has shown the importance of leaders
for our society (e.g. Besley et al., 2011; Jones and Olken,
2005). In practice, although politicians are chosen by voters,
in most cases a selection is made by political parties “that act
as gate-keepers to aspiring candidates” (Buisseret, 2022: 1).
In other words, political parties shape the possible choices
for voters, inﬂuence the paths of those who decide to build a
political career and, indirectly, the interests most likely to be
heard (Crotty, 1968). Moreover, their choice may not co-
incide with the voter’s preference for candidates (Casey
et al., 2021), and they may even resort to unwanted oli-
garchical practices such as nepotism (Michels, 1915).
In this article we shed new light on the parties’ selection
process, which was once labeled the “secret garden of pol-
itics” (Gallagher and Marsh, 1988). We study a case of open-
list proportional representation (OLPR), using data for
Brazilian local legislative elections in 2012 and 2016. These
elections are the traditional launching pad for a political
career in Brazil (Lima and Barreto, 2014; Messias, 2019;
Miguel, 2003; Noll and Leal, 2008). To improve our un-
derstanding of party selection, we examine what happens
when parties can include more candidates on their party lists.
Who are the candidates who will occupy the additional seats
(henceforth “marginal candidates”)? How can the availability
of longer lists affect the political careers of newcomers?
These are difﬁcult questions to answer because there are
several empirical challenges. First, the number of candi-
dates is deﬁned by the political parties themselves, which
raises concerns about endogeneity. Second, unlike a closed-
list proportional representation systems (CLPR) where the
ranking of candidates is explicit, in an open-list system we
observe only the candidates selected by the party, but not
their ranking or who was left out. We use Brazilian mu-
nicipal elections as our laboratory, as they offer a quasi-
experimental variation in the maximum list size that allows
us to overcome the above-mentioned challenges.

Our research design explores the fact that in Brazil the
maximum size of party lists is a function of the district
magnitude, which in turn is a non-linear function of the
municipalities’ population. Therefore, we use a Regression
Discontinuity Design (RDD) approach that estimates in the
effects of the population discontinuities on several out-
comes, such as the number of candidates, list size, list
composition and election outcomes. Our paper can be seen
as the ﬁrst attempt at using causal inference to understand
the effect of increases in maximum allowed size of party
lists, on election results and the future career of newcomers
to the political arena.
In Brazil, party leaders face a trade-off as they com-
pose their candidate lists (Cheibub and Sin, 2020). On the
one hand, they target candidates with the best potential to
maximize their total number of votes, since the number of
seats a party obtains depends on the sum of the votes of its
candidates. On the other hand, parties avoid including too
many competitive candidates so as to prevent the eruption
of an internal war. Therefore, our hypothesis is that
parties will choose intermediate candidates, generating an
inverted-U-shaped relationship between expected voting
and the probability of being selected as the marginal
candidate.
We split the candidates into two groups: incumbents and
other politicians who have already won an election
(“winners”), which are deﬁned as “strong”, while new-
comers and veteran politicians who have never won an
election (“losers”) are grouped as “weak”. According to our
hypothesis, strong candidates should not occupy marginal
seats, as they are candidates with high potential for at-
tracting votes away from the local party leaders. In addition,
the weakest candidates are not observed, as they are not
even selected to compete for the marginal seats. Our ex-
pectation is that these will be allocated to “intermediate”
candidates, those with an electoral potential that is big
enough to contribute to the party, but not too big to threaten
the position of the party leaders.
Our ﬁndings show that, as population increases dis-
continuously, the number of candidates running for ofﬁce
increases by about 6 (or by 6% of the total number of
candidates). This increase is driven by the expansion in
party lists, with no impact on the number of lists. Con-
ﬁrming our expectations, almost all the marginal candi-
dates on party lists are “weak” and among them more than
70% are newcomers. Looking at the composition of the
extra seat after the election, there is a 65–80% chance that
it will be occupied by a “weak” candidate, more likely a
newcomer. Thus, the increase in the list size not only
increases the participation of newcomers, but also their
chance of winning a seat. With regards to the medium-
term effects, our estimates indicate that the chance of a
newcomer who was defeated in t being elected in t + 4 is
signiﬁcantly higher in the treated municipalities (roughly
20% increase). A similar gain is not seen for the other
groups of candidates, which indicates that the experience
provided by the ﬁrst election is important for electoral
success (Haime et al., 2022). Our estimates are robust to
several robustness tests. In terms of minorities, we ﬁnd a
positive impact on women’s participation, but only due to
the institutional requirement of a gender quota, which
suggests that parties act as “minimal compliers” (Lucardi
and Micozzi, 2022). We ﬁnd no effects on the candidacies
of blacks. Moreover, no impact was identiﬁed on the
election of women and blacks.
This study is related to several strands of the literature. Our
results contribute to the debate on the degree of intra-party
competition in an OLPR system. Although some papers argue
that in this electoral system there is a high level of intra-party
competition
(Ames,
1995;
Power,
1991),
our
quasi-
experimental evidence aligns with recent papers which
challenge this view, showing that intra-party competition
system in an OLPR is constrained (Cheibub and Sin, 2020;
Zucco and Power, 2021). Differently from these studies,
however, our focus is on the lower government level
(councilors) and not that of the federal level (deputies). Our
results also challenge the conventional view that Brazilian
parties are of little relevance, given their strategic control over
lists. In this sense, we also speak to the literature that studies
the composition of party lists (Buisseret, 2022; Ceyhan, 2018;
Cox et al., 2021; Svit´akov´a and Solt´es, 2020), more specif-
ically those referring to OLPR in a weakly institutionalized
party system (e.g. Samuels, 2003; Scully, 1995).
This paper adds to the growing literature on political
selection which investigates how the institutional envi-
ronment shapes the quality of the political class, placing
political parties at the center of the decision-making
process (Galasso and Nannicini, 2011, 2017; Mattozzi
and Merlo, 2015). To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst paper
relating the size of the candidate lists to the party’s se-
lection of candidates and to entering a political career,
using an exogenous variation in list size, which allows
one to identify causal effects. Our results indicate that the
list size impacts the composition of candidate lists, fa-
voring the entry of less competitive candidates. On an-
alyzing the starting point of a political career in Brazil,
our analysis contributes to the literature investigating
entrance to a political career, by showing that the extra
seat allows more newcomers to enter politics, and even to
be elected, whether on their ﬁrst attempt or their second
(Fox and Lawless, 2005; Gulzar, 2021; Schlesinger,
1966).
The study begins describing the data and institutional
framework. Then we provide a brief overview of the
composition of candidate lists and candidacy hierarchy. In a
subsequent section empirical strategy is discussed. Then we
presents the estimation results and conclude with the
broader implications of our ﬁndings.

Institutional framework and data
This section brieﬂy describes the main features of Brazil’s
electoral system. We then discuss how the size of can-
didate lists is determined in Brazilian municipalities.
Finally, we present the data used, as well as some de-
scriptive statistics.
Municipal elections and seat distribution in Brazil
Brazil is a federal republic in a presidential system, with
three levels of government, namely federal, state of which
there are 26 (plus the Federal District, where the capital
Bras´ılia is located), and more than 5,500 municipalities. Just
as at state and federal levels, municipalities are governed by
an executive and a legislature, in this case a mayor and
councilors, respectively. Municipal elections take place
every 4 years and electors vote for a mayor (prefeito) and a
councilor (vereador).1
In the case of the legislature, according to Electoral Law
9,504/1997, each party proposes an open list of up to 1.5*M
candidates, where M is the district magnitude - that is crucial
for our identiﬁcation strategy. For municipalities with fewer
than 100,000 voters, parties can form coalitions and then
jointly present a list of 2*M candidates, a strategy adopted
by most parties in Brazil, especially small ones (Calvo et al.,
2015).2 To simplify the exposition, we will not distinguish
these lists, naming them only as candidate lists.
Voters cast a single vote either for the entire party list
(list-vote) or for a party candidate (preferential vote). These
votes are pooled and the allocation of seats takes place in
two steps: ﬁrst, based on the electoral quotient (a district’s
total valid votes divided by district magnitude) and on the
party quotient (a party’s total valid votes divided by elec-
toral quotient), the seats are allocated between the parties.
Second, the remaining seats are allocated based on the
average criterion (a party’s total valid votes + number of
vacant seats won by the average criterion + 1). After de-
ﬁning the allocation between parties, the allocation within
parties are made. Brazil uses an open-list system, so that if
the party wins N seats, they are allocated to the N candidates
most voted by the party. There is no limit to the number of
terms a councilor can serve. In Appendix A the distribution
of seats is exempliﬁed by a hypothetical election.
In addition to being the basis of the political hierarchy in
Brazil, local councils are in many cases the start of poli-
ticians careers (Lima and Barreto, 2014; Messias, 2019;
Miguel, 2003; Noll and Leal, 2008), which is why they
receive special attention in the literature.3 As deﬁned by the
Brazilian Constitution, it is up to councilors to submit and
approve municipal laws, and monitor the work of the ex-
ecutive. In addition, it is common for them to respond
directly to voters’ demands, whether personal or community
(Altmann, 2010; Lopez, 2004).
Populational discontinuities and size of
candidate lists
The basis of our identiﬁcation strategy is the way in which
maximum size of candidate lists is determined. As we have
seen, they are deﬁned by the magnitude of the district. The
size of a municipal council was established by Constitutional
Amendment 58/2009, known as the Councilors’ Constitu-
tional Amendment Proposal. From the 2012 municipal
elections onwards, 24 population brackets were established
with their respective municipal council size caps. The ﬁrst
group comprises small municipalities of up to 15,000 in-
habitants with a limit of up to nine councilors, while the last
bracket (55 councilors) is for municipalities with more than
eight million inhabitants – which applies only to the city of
São Paulo. With emphasis on the ﬁrst six discontinuities, with
15,000, 30,000, 50,000, 80,000, 120,000 and 160,000 in-
habitants, the council size cap increases to 11, 13, 15, 17,
19 and 21, respectively.4
As the maximum size of candidate lists is twice the
number of local councilors, for the aforementioned dis-
continuities the cap will be 18, 22, 26, 30, 34, 38 and 42.5
Table 1 summarizes the mapping of population intervals and
the constitutional cap for candidate lists size. This table also
shows that many parties/coalitions chose to have less
candidates than the constitutional cap. Our study analyzes
the most recent population rule, established for the 2012 and
2016 elections.
One concern involving these discontinuities refers to
simultaneity with any other population-based policies
(Eggers et al., 2018). This problem occurs at the population
threshold of 50,000 inhabitants, when there is both an in-
crease in lists size (from 26 to 30) and an increase in
councilors’ wage cap (Ferraz and Finan, 2009), which could
lead to an identiﬁcation problem. To overcome this prob-
lem, we also assess the impact of list size excluding the
municipalities around this cutoff.

OLPR, list composition and
candidacy hierarchy
Candidate lists in Brazil
One of the main distinctions between electoral systems is
the division between majoritarian elections and propor-
tional representation. This is based on the principle that
seats are allocated between parties in proportion to the
share of votes they receive. Proportional representation
systems are diverse as to the way in which the n seats of
the parties are allocated among their candidates. At one
extreme is the CLPR, where before elections parties rank
candidates in their list, so that voters vote for the parties
and the top candidates are those who win the seats. At the
other extreme is the OLPR system, in which voters cast a
ballot for an individual candidate and the number of votes
determines the top party candidates who will win those
seats.
It is widely accepted that in open-list systems the per-
sonal reputation of candidates is more relevant than in a
closed-list system (Ames, 1995; Carey and Shugart, 1995;
Sartori, 1976). The OLPR is the system prevailing in Brazil
and seems to be characterized both by individualism and an
underdeveloped
party
system
(e.g.
Kinzo,
2005;
Mainwaring, 1991; Scully, 1995; Samuels, 1997, 2003),
with a high level of fragmentation and party switching
(Desposato, 2006). The combination of individualism and
weak political parties seems to imply campaigns focused on
candidates, with a high level of intra-party competition
(Ames, 1995; Power, 1991).

However, recent studies have challenged the view that
Brazilian political parties are of little relevance and that
intra-party competition is rampant (Cheibub and Sin, 2015,
2020; Silotto, 2019; Zucco and Power, 2021). First, al-
though the open-list system reduces the power of parties vis-
`a-vis the closed-list system, lists in Brazil are not as open as
in other OLPRs, as parties act as gatekeepers, centralizing
both the decision on the composition of lists and the al-
location of campaign resources (Braga, 2008; Guarnieri,
2004; Meira, 2020; Ribeiro, 2013). Second, both candidates
and parties want to limit internal competition, as candidates
do not want to face difﬁculties in elections, such as disputes
for the constituency and for the party’s resources, while the
parties want to prevent good candidates from switching to
other parties which might offer them better opportunities
(Carroll and Nalepa, 2020; Cheibub and Sin, 2015, 2020;
Mershon, 2020; Silotto, 2019). Thus, lists are strategically
constructed to prevent intra-party competition (Cheibub and
Sin, 2015).
Parties face a trade-off when composing their lists. On
the one hand, they want to have competitive candidates to
gain more votes and thus more seats. But, on the other hand,
parties must avoid including many competitive candidates
so as to prevent the eruption of an internal war. To solve this
internal conﬂict, one strategy would be to estimate how
many seats they expect to win and include in their ﬁnal list a
number of competitive candidates close to that number
(Cheibub and Sin, 2015). The remaining seats would be
occupied by less competitive candidates, who can still
obtain a signiﬁcant number of votes to contribute to the
party reaching the electoral quotient (total valid votes di-
vided by the number of seats).
Therefore, the ﬁrst step would be to ﬁnd candidates with
great electoral potential to guarantee access to the legisla-
ture, a procedure that has been widely documented in
studies
on
political
recruitment
in
Brazilian
parties
(Altmann, 2010; Bolognesi, 2013; Braga and Amaral, 2013;
Braga and Veiga, 2009; Marques, 2007). There is a vast
literature highlighting the importance of political experience
(i.e., ofﬁce-holding experience and incumbency) for a
candidate’s electoral success. In particular, candidates who
have already been successfully tested at the polls have the
greatest electoral potential (Cirone et al., 2021; Fiva and
Røhr, 2018), especially in Brazil, given the high levels of
incumbency advantage of candidates in the legislature
(Avelino et al., 2022; Meira, 2020; Meireles, 2019). In
addition to incumbents, candidates with ofﬁce-holding
experience (winners) also have electoral advantages, even
when running for different political ofﬁces (multi-ofﬁce
incumbency advantage, see Magalhaes and Hirvonen
(2015)). Thus, such candidates “tend to be favored by
the political parties when they face the ﬁnal ﬁlter of the
elections” (Perissinotto and Bolognesi, 2010: 12). Such
evidence suggests the primacy of incumbents and winners
during the composition of party lists, so, as suggested by
Cheibub and Sin (2020), we will classify them as strong
candidates.
Moreover, candidate experience is also positively cor-
related with electoral success (Anagol and Fujiwara, 2016;
Górecki and Kukołowicz, 2014). Haime et al. (2022) ﬁnds
that the candidate experience may improve electoral success
for some reasons: (i) they learn how to run campaign for
political ofﬁce; (ii) it provides candidates with a network of
politicians, donors and supporters who can help in subse-
quent elections; (iii) it improves their visibility among the
electorate. Cheibub and Sin (2020) also documents a good
electoral performance of past unsuccessful candidates in
Brazil, especially those who obtained a signiﬁcant vote in
the past. Finally, the candidates with the least potential are
those with no previous candidate experience, the so-called
newcomers. To simplify the analysis, we will group losers
and newcomers as weak candidates. Figure 2 summarizes
the classiﬁcation of candidates according to their electoral
experience.
Candidacy hierarchy
One way to ﬁnd out how parties compose their lists is by
checking what happens when they are allowed to include
more candidates on the party lists. Who will be the marginal
candidates? As we have seen, parties are careful to prevent
the entry of additional strong candidates, while it is inter-
esting for them to include candidates who can contribute to
increase the electoral quotient.
If EðVÞ is the expected vote of a candidate, there must be
a minimum expected vote ðEðVÞMinÞ so that for a vote lower
than this value it is not worthwhile for the party to include
the candidate on its list, as it could waste resources without
making a relevant contribution. On the other hand, there is a
maximum expected vote ðEðVÞMaxÞ beyond which the
candidate becomes a threat to the current candidates. For
example, in the 2008 municipal elections in the municipality
of Pelotas, party leaders report that, while incumbents are
almost automatically included in the list, the search for the
electoral quotient means that the remaining seats are ﬁlled
by candidates with some electoral potential, as long as these
emerging candidates do not threaten the election of es-
tablished candidates (Altmann, 2010).
Thus, our expectation is that marginal candidates
(i.e., those who are likely to occupy the additional seats) will
have an intermediate electoral potential. This idea is il-
lustrated in Figure 3, which shows that there is an inverted-
U relationship between expected voting and the probability
of being selected as the marginal candidate.
If we consider that there are four types of candidates,
incumbents (I), winners (W), losers (L) and newcomers (N),
parties can rank candidates in accordance with their ex-
pected votes. According to the previous discussion,

Using the terminology of Cirone et al. (2021) and Cox
et al. (2021), there are three types of positions in the
candidates ranking: (i) safe spots: the candidate is in-
cluded in the list regardless of whether there are more
available slots; (ii) hot spots: intermediate position, the
candidate is on the margin to enter the ﬁnal list, but only if
a few more slots are added; and, (iii) hopeless spots:
lower rankings, candidates who would never enter the
party’s ﬁnal list. Thus, on average, the I type should be in
the safe spots as they have the greatest electoral potential,
while the L and N types are over-represented in the
hopeless spots.
For example, consider a municipality with four coun-
cilors, where the coalition ﬁlls the list with all possible slots
(t). If, as Brazilian electoral legislation establishes, the
maximum size of the candidate list is 2 times the number of
councilors, then t = 8. Suppose there is an increase in the
number of councilors to six, so that in the new list t0 = 12, so
that four new candidates can be included in the ﬁnal list,
occupying the hot spots. A hypothetical party list with
17 pre-candidates is depicted in Figure 4. Note that, as
described in the model, the ranking of candidates is cor-
related to their expected votes, so that candidates of type I
are on average better ranked, while N are in more mar-
ginalized positions. In this example, 3/4 of the additional
slots are ﬁlled by N, while the remaining 1/4 is ﬁlled by L. In
short, the additional seat is not ﬁlled by the most com-
petitive candidates because (1) as they are a priority on the
party list, they will be on the ﬁnal list regardless of whether
or not there are more seats; (2) the party wants to limit
internal conﬂicts, so it allocates new seats to less com-
petitive candidates.
Our focus in this paper is to investigate the impact of an
increase in the size of the lists on the composition of these
lists, on the political career of newcomers and on election
outcomes. We use a regression discontinuity (RD) ap-
proach, to be described in the next section, which allows us
to identify the appropriate counterfactual using the pop-
ulation discontinuities that deﬁne the caps on size of can-
didate lists.
Econometric strategy
RD design
Our main objective is to identify the impact of the size of
candidate lists on the composition of candidates available to
voters. The institutional framework mandates that list size is
determined by the council size, which is a non-linear
function of the municipality population, that is, the maxi-
mum list size changes discontinuously along the population
thresholds. This exogenous source of variation induced by
the institutional setup ﬁts into a RD design, which will allow
us to identify the effect of interest.
The RD design exploits the discontinuity in the assignment
to the treatment to identify the causal effect (Imbens and
Lemieux, 2008). The treatment assignment Ti,t is one if mu-
nicipality i in election t fall above the cutoff, that is, increases
the maximum size of the candidate list. If this increase is the

(Gelman and Imbens, 2019). As Imbens and Lemieux
(2008) suggests, we use a simple rectangular (or uni-
form) kernel. Following Corbi et al. (2019), we also control
for year-state ﬁxed effects (δts) to account for regional
business cycles, and for year-cutoff ﬁxed effects to deal with
different trends across municipalities of different sizes. The
inference is based on heteroskedasticity-robust standard
errors clustered at municipal level (ui,t). The β parameter
estimate the impact of variations implied by the lists size
caps on several outcomes, such as number of candidates,
actual list sizes, list composition and council composition
after the election.
The main limitation of our empirical strategy is that, parallel
to the increase in the size of the candidate lists, there is an
increase in the magnitude of the district. Since it is not possible
to evaluate the effects of district size and of candidate lists
separately, as the maximum list size is a deterministic function of
district magnitude close to the discontinuities, we can only
evaluate the overall effect of discontinuity on the composition of
marginal candidates. According to Lundell (2004) and Shomer
(2014), there is no evidence that district magnitude impacts the
candidate selection process, so we assume that the effects of
district magnitude on the composition of the candidate list and
political career are of second order (such as changing turnout
(Lyytik¨ainen and Tukiainen, 2019), corruption (Britto and
Fiorin, 2020) and expenditure (Avis et al., 2022)).
To deal with the trade-off between bias and variance, we
show estimations using different bandwidths. A narrow
bandwidth uses only observations relatively close to
thresholds, where local randomization of the treatment is
most likely. As suggested by Litschig and Morrison (2013)
and Corbi et al. (2019), we adopt relative bandwidths for
each of the thresholds (h = 20%, h = 10% and h = 5%), that
is, we only use a neighborhood that is given a percentage of
distance from each threshold.9
Figure 5 illustrates the estimation approach for pop-
ulation discontinuities. In general, the municipalities are
allocated to the population threshold that is closest to them.
If the nearest threshold is on the right, then the municipality
belongs to the control group for this threshold. If the closest
is on the left, then the municipality belongs to the treatment
group for this threshold. For example, all municipalities
with less than 15,000 inhabitants (whose cap is 18) are
potentially the control group of the ﬁrst threshold.10 For
municipalities between 15,001 and 30,000 inhabitants
(whose cap is 22), there are those which are closest to
the ﬁrst threshold (15,001–22,500) and those closest to the
second

from

from the treatment group of the ﬁrst threshold. This rea-
soning applies to all thresholds.
The ﬁrst identiﬁcation hypothesis requires continuity of
pre-treatment characteristics relevant to the outcome vari-
ables around the thresholds. Appendix Table C.1 assesses
the impact of the population thresholds on 12 municipal
covariates. No pre-treatment covariates display systematic
discontinuities around the thresholds, which means that the
pre-treatment
covariates are balanced, increasing
the

It is also essential that local governments do not manipulate
their population count (Eggers et al., 2018). We evaluate
whether there is a discontinuity in the density of the running
variable (population) along the thresholds, an indication that
the municipalities manipulate the population count in order to
increase lists size. If there is precise manipulation of pop-
ulation estimates and this correlates to the outcomes, then our
estimates do not identify the causal effect.

Results
In this section, we ﬁrst estimate the impact of the change in
the constitutional cap of candidate lists on the size and
composition of the candidates list, as well as on current and
future election outcomes. In addition, we also check
whether the possibility of larger candidate lists impact the
participation of minority groups in politics. As described in
equation (1), all speciﬁcations include a ﬁrst-order poly-
nomial of normalized population. To check if our estimates
are sensitive to the choice of bandwidth, the neighborhood
of population thresholds is restricted to three relative
bandwidths: 20%, 10% and 5%. In addition, a vector of
observed municipal characteristics, ﬁxed year-state and
year-cutoff effects are included.
In addition to the estimated coefﬁcients, the mean of the
dependent variable in the control group (10% bandwidth) is
presented in Column 1. In Columns 2, 3 and 4, the sample is
restricted to bandwidths of 20%, 10% and 5%, respectively.
In Column 5 we present the ratio between the estimate of
Column 3 and the mean presented in Column 1.
Effects of the list size on the composition
of candidacies
This section presents the main econometric results on the
effect of the list size cap on political candidacies. As the
number of candidates that parties can include in their list
changes discontinuously along the populational thresholds
(2 * number of councilors), a positive impact on the total
number of candidates and the size of the lists is expected.
But, is this increase in the number of candidate solely due to
the increase in the size of candidate lists, or does rising
district magnitude lead to an increasing number of lists as
well? There is mixed empirical evidence regarding the
impact of district magnitude on the number of parties (e.g.
Lewis, 2018; Lucardi, 2019; Singer and Gershman, 2018).
Calvo et al. (2015) ﬁnds that in the Brazilian Congress it is
common to form coalitions composed of a large number of
parties, with the existence of pro-small party biases. In this
context, the number of lists should not be sensitive to the
magnitude of the district.
Table 2 presents the main results. The ﬁrst row shows
that in discontinuity there is a signiﬁcant increase in the total
number of candidacies: the estimated treatment effect is
approximately 5.1–6.2 new candidates, which corresponds
to a relative increase of 5.1–6.2%. Additionally, we see that
the increase in candidates is due to expansion in list size,
about 0.9–1.2, with no effect on the number of candidate
lists. Note that, as in discontinuity the candidate lists can be
expanded by four candidates (See Section Institutional
Framework and Data), the increase in candidate lists by 0.9–
1.2 is equivalent to an increase of 0.2–0.3 for each increase
in the constitutional cap.

Conclusion
This paper provides new evidence on how parties allocate
additional slots on the party list in an open-list system, with
a focus on candidates’ political-electoral careers. Using data
from the 2012 and 2016 Brazilian municipal elections, we
apply a RDD when analyzing an exogenous variation in the
maximum number of candidates that a party can select.
Using data on the number of candidates and their electoral
background, we ﬁnd that additional slots allow parties to
select more marginal candidates for their lists, which im-
plies a greater proportion of weak candidates. This result
lends support to the hypothesis that in an OLPR system,
parties control intra-party competition during the con-
struction of their lists (e.g. Cheibub and Sin, 2020).
In addition to expanding their share among candidates,
the results show that weak candidates, mainly newcomers,
occupy a signiﬁcant share of the additional seats. After
expanding the horizon of the analysis, we ﬁnd that, even
when defeated in their ﬁrst attempt, the electoral experience
obtained by novices is important for their success in a future
election. Finally, the additional seat did not manage to
broaden the share of minorities in politics.
Our results leave gaps to be ﬁlled in future work. First, if
the new slots are divided between candidates with no
previous electoral experience, who were these newcomers
before being nominated? In other words, were they mem-
bers of the public service? Is this an intermediate step
between afﬁliation and candidacy? Finally, our results are
valid for a given context, namely, Brazilian municipalities in
an OLPR system. New studies in other countries and
contexts could provide more robust evidence about the rules
for party selection.

1. The federal capital, Bras´ılia, and the Fernando de Noronha
archipelago are the only two municipalities which do not hold
municipal elections, and are not, therefore, considered in any
of the analysis which follows.
2. After the period of analysis of this article (2012 and 2016)
some important changes were made in the Brazilian electoral
legislation. A constitutional reform in 2017 banned coalitions
for the position of councilor, so that for municipal elections
from 2020 onwards only party lists are allowed. Another
change was introduced by the Law n°14,211/2021, which
reduced the maximum size of party lists to M + 1.
3. Although there is no rigidity in political careers in Brazil, it is
common for individuals to make their debut in politics in
higher positions, such as those of mayor, state and federal
deputies, for example.
4. As in studies by Brollo et al. (2013) and Corbi et al. (2019),
our analysis focuses on the ﬁrst discontinuities which, in our
case cover more than 97.5% of all Brazilian municipalities.
5. As mentioned, coalitions can present lists whose maximum
size is twice the magnitude of the district for municipalities
with less than 100,000 voters. As they represent more than
99% of our sample, to simplify our exposition we will em-
phasize this case.
6. Electoral data were collected from https://dadosabertos.tse.
jus.br/ (accessed on 23/07/2022).
7. The average size of the candidate lists is obtained by dividing
the number of candidates by the number of lists submitted.
8. Several studies on the closed-list system, where the ordering
of the party list is explicitly observable, point to an ordering of
the lists according to the candidate’s electoral potential
(Buisseret, 2022; Ceyhan, 2018; Cox et al., 2021; Svit´akov´a
and Solt´es, 2020). In the open-list system, we do not observe
the ordering carried out by the parties, but only the names of
the pre-candidates included in the ﬁnal list.
9. As Corbi et al. (2019) points out, the relative bandwidth
approach is preferable to the absolute in order to avoid losing
many observations as the population increases. For example, a
10% bandwidth at the ﬁrst threshold (15,000) will include
municipalities with populations between 13,500 and 16,500
inhabitants, while at the second threshold (30,000) we have
municipalities with populations between 27,000 and 33,000.
10. Depending on the bandwidth adopted, only municipalities
relatively close to the threshold are included in the sample.
