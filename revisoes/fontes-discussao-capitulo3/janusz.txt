Article
Political parties and campaign
resource allocation: Gender
gaps in Brazilian elections
Andrew Janusz
, Sofi-Nicole Barreiro and Erika Cintron
University of Florida, USA
Abstract
Political parties shape electoral outcomes by determining who stands for election and what campaign resources they have
at their disposal. The introduction of gender quotas have led party leaders to nominate more women candidates,
however, those women disproportionately lose. We contend that one of the reasons that women routinely lose is
because party elites withhold the campaign resources necessary to mount an effective campaign. In this paper, we test
this resource gatekeeping argument using data on the provision of campaign resources in Brazil. We analyze the
distribution of three different types of party resources: candidate identification numbers, financial support, and
television airtime. Our findings show that party elites provide female candidates less advantageous candidate
identification numbers, less financial support, and less media access than they provide male candidates. Importantly, we
do not find that gender gaps in campaign contributions are attributable to differences in candidate quality. This finding
suggests that even when women are recruited to run for office, party elites may still undermine their electoral prospects.
Keywords
Brazil, campaigns, elections, gender, political parties
Introduction
Political parties play an important role in determining who
stands for election. Through selective recruitment and
nomination procedures, they shape the composition of can-
didate fields, and therein, elected assemblies (Gallagher
and Marsh, 1988; Norris and Lovenduski, 1995; Siavelis,
2012). Historically, party leaders’ gatekeeping behavior
has impeded women from attaining representation com-
mensurate with their proportion in society (Bjarnega˚rd and
Kenny, 2015; Hinojosa, 2012; Lawless and Fox, 2010). To
combat these long-standing disparities, feminists and pro-
gressive politicians have pushed for the introduction of
gender quotas (Franceschet et al., 2012; Krook, 2010).
Where successful, the impact of gender quotas, which
ensure that political parties nominate a minimum number
of women to run for office, has been dramatic (Jones, 2009;
Tripp and Kang, 2008). In the past 25 years, women’s
representation in national legislatures around the world has
more than doubled (IPU, 2019).
In Latin America, the widespread adoption of gender
quotas led party elites to nominate more women candidates.
Nevertheless, women have achieved greater political
representation in some countries than in others (Schwindt-
Bayer, 2009). Brazil, for example, lags other countries in
the region and the world with respect to women’s political
representation (dos Santos and Wylie, 2018). Although at
least 30% of the candidates that parties nominate for con-
gressional office must be female, as of 2018, only 14.6%
of seats in Brazil’s lower house of Congress are held by
women.
In this article, we contend that even when party gate-
keepers nominate women to run for office, they may
impede them from winning public office by withholding
critical campaign resources. We test this argument using
data on the distribution of campaign resources in Brazil,
where party elites have discretion over three types of
campaign resources: candidate identification numbers,
financial resources, and broadcast media time. We find that

To assess whether the distribution of campaign

impact of campaign resources on electoral outcomes. Using

improve women’s electoral prospects. Controlling for cam-

receive significantly fewer votes than men. This suggests
that eliminating resource gaps is not panacea.
Scholars have traditionally pointed to gendered selec-
tion processes as hindering women at the nomination stage
from becoming candidates. Gatekeeping by party elites,
however, does not only occur at the nomination stage. Party
elites also shape electoral outcomes by allocating campaign
resources. In this article, we show that when gender quotas
require parties to nominate women, elites may use their
discretion over campaign resources to perpetuate the polit-
ical power of men.
Political parties and women’s
representation
Political parties are central to electoral politics. They shape
who runs for elected office via recruitment and selection
procedures (Lawless and Fox, 2010; Norris, 1997; Norris
and Lovenduski, 1995; Siavelis, 2012). Parties’ control
over candidate selection processes has not escaped the
attention of gender politics scholars. A substantial body
of research shows that political parties in western democ-
racies recruit men more often and more intensely than
women (Fox and Lawless, 2010; Lawless and Fox, 2010).
One of the principal explanations for this finding is that
party elites, who are overwhelmingly male, recruit candi-
dates from their own male-dominated networks (Butler and
Preece, 2016; Crowder-Meyer, 2013; Hinojosa, 2012; San-
bonmatsu, 2010).
The introduction of gender quotas has forced party elites
to broaden their recruiting efforts (Barnes and Holman,
2020; Hinojosa, 2012; Hinojosa and V´azquez, 2018). Yet
evidence shows that political parties routinely nominate the
minimal number of women required by law (Bjarnega˚rd
and Zetterberg, 2016; Htun and Jones, 2002; Piscopo,
2016; Wylie et al., 2019). Moreover, when quota laws “lack
teeth,” parties routinely fail to even nominate the required
number of women (Gatto, 2016; Gatto and Wylie, 2021;
Hinojosa and Piscopo, 2013; Llanos and Sample, 2008;
Wylie and dos Santos, 2016).
Monitoring compliance and punishing violations may
deter party elites from engaging in this type of gendered
gatekeeping behavior. Nonetheless, even when political
parties comply with gender quotas and nominate women
to run for office, the percentage of women elected to public
office routinely lags the rates at which they run (Rosen,
2013). One potential reason that women continue to be
descriptively underrepresented in democracies with gender
quotas is that nomination is necessary, but not sufficient.
Female candidates, like their male counterparts, require
campaign resources.
Quota laws require political parties to nominate women,
but do not compel party elites to provide those female
candidates the campaign resources necessary to run com-
petitive electoral campaigns. If a political party nominates
a candidate but does not provide her the necessary
resources to run a competitive campaign, she is in effect
a “sacrificial lamb,” a party standard-bearer with no real
shot at winning public office (Thomas and Bodet, 2013).
Political parties and the distribution of
campaign resources
Modern democracy is unthinkable without political parties
(Schattschneider, 1960). They aggregate interests, provide
voters a meaningful choice at the ballot box, and when
successful, govern. Parties must assemble a winning coali-
tion to convert their supporters’ preferences into policy.
Minimum winning coalitions are most conducive to achiev-
ing this goal (Riker, 1962). To ensure that the party has the
requisite numbers to pass preferred legislation, party leader
aide affiliated candidates in winning elected office by pro-
viding them campaign resources. Party leaders have discre-
tion over a variety of different types of campaign resources.
They can facilitate financial contributions from donors
within the party network, and, at times, contribute directly
to candidates’ campaigns (Avelino and Fisch, 2018; Has-
sell, 2016; Jacobson, 1985; Kolodny, 1998; Ocampo,
2018). Moreover, party elites routinely help them assemble
a professional campaign staff, provide information about
the electorate and their competitors, as well as assist them
attaining media exposure (Fraga and Hassell, 2018; Has-
sell, 2018; Hassell and Visalvanich, 2019). In some
instances, political parties are, in fact, the sole providers
of media exposure. For example, in some Latin American
and Western European countries, paid political advertising
on television is prohibited, and party elites have discretion
over which candidates receive publicity in state-sponsored
advertising segments (Casas-Zamora, 2016).
Party elites are presumed to distribute campaign
resources efficiently to maximize their party’s seats in

parliament. Consistent with this logic, political parties in
the United States routinely distribute funds to vulnerable
incumbents and qualified challengers (Damore and Hans-
ford, 1999; Herrnson, 2009). Yet an individual candidate’s
chances of electoral success in contested races is never
known with certainty. This is especially true in multi-
member electoral districts, which tend to attract larger
candidate fields. In those situations, party elites’ resource
allocation decisions are likely to depend on subjective
assessments of candidates’ abilities.
Information on candidate background and electoral
experience is likely to have an outsized impact on elite
perceptions of electoral potential. Since incumbents win
reelection with remarkable frequency (Jacobson, 1985),
party elites are likely to perceive them as the strongest
candidates and privilege them over novices when distribut-
ing resources (Jacobson, 1993). Similarly, candidates who
have previously held elected public office are likely to be
considered strong candidates and receive party support
(Cheibub and Sin, 2020). Party elites may also favor expe-
rienced politicians for policy as opposed to electoral rea-
sons. Previous office holders have social connections,
policy expertise, and practical knowledge of government
that can help the party accomplish their legislative agenda
(Norris and Lovenduski, 1995). This leads to the following
hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1: Party elites provide experienced politi-
cians greater campaign resources than inexperienced
candidates.
Electoral experience, though, may not be the only factor
that shapes party elites’ resource allocation decisions.
Given the substantial research which shows that party elites
favor men over women in recruitment, we might also
expect party elites to favor men when allocating resources.
This may occur for two different reasons. First, party elites,
who are overwhelmingly male, may believe that the ideal
candidates possess characteristics like their own. Niven
(1998) shows that male party leaders express a preference
for individuals with traditionally masculine traits. Those
deemed to be competitive, outspoken and individualistic,
were perceived as preferable to those that were cautious
and compassionate. It is also possible that party elites might
provide resources disproportionately to male candidates
because they believe that members of the electorate prefer
males.
A growing body of survey and experimental work indi-
cates that voters are willing to support female candidates
(Aguilar et al., 2015; Burrell, 1996; Dolan, 2014; Lawless,
2004; Schwarz et al., 2018; Seltzer et al., 1997; Wylie,
2018). Nevertheless, there is still a widespread belief that
voters prefer male candidates (Bateson, 2020; Lawless and
Fox, 2010; McElroy and Marsh, 2011; Sanbonmatsu,
2006). Sanbonmatsu (2006) finds that in the U.S. some
party leaders are overly pessimistic about women’s
chances. If party elites believe that female candidates will
face an electoral penalty, they may be less willing to pro-
vide them campaign resources. We expect that:
Hypothesis 2: Party elites privilege men over women in
the distribution of campaign resources.
Brazil presents an ideal case to test these hypotheses.
Due to gender quotas, at least 30% of the candidates that
political parties nominate to run for congress must be
women. However, parties are not required to provide the
women they nominate the campaign resources needed to
mount effective electoral campaigns.
Brazil’s electoral system, political parties,
and campaign resource provision
Brazil is known for having a candidate-centric electoral
system (Shugart, 2005). Aspiring politicians running for a
seat in the Chamber of Deputies, Brazil’s 513 seat lower
house of Congress, compete in statewide electoral dis-
tricts under open-list proportional representation (OLPR)
rules. Because candidates compete against candidates
from other political parties as well as copartisans, they
run personalistic campaigns (Ames, 1995; Carey and Shu-
gart, 1995). Candidates highlight their personal character-
istics and seldom mention their party affiliation (Samuels,
2001b).
Brazilian voters routinely have an abundance of candi-
dates to choose from. Thousands of candidates typically
run for congressional office (Cunow et al., 2021). Voters
are entitled to cast a single ballot either for a political
party or an individual candidate. Most voters, though, cast
a preference vote for a particular candidate (Avelino and
Fisch, 2018). When polls close on election day, votes cast
for individual candidates are pooled at the party level and
seats within the legislature are then allocated to parties
according to the proportion of all valid votes they receive.
Which candidates receive those seats depends on how
they perform relative to the other candidates on the party
list.
Brazilian candidates seek a “personal vote” in an effort
to win office (Ames, 1995; Carey and Shugart, 1995). In
their campaign advertisements they highlight their qualifi-
cations and policy preference and seldom mention their
party affiliation (Ames, 1995; Carey and Shugart, 1995;
Samuels, 2001a). Although candidates run personalistic
campaigns, political parties are by no means electorally
irrelevant. Brazilian party elites have discretion over the
distribution of three types of campaign resources: candidate
identification numbers, campaign funds, and broadcast
airtime.

Candidate identification numbers
To cast their vote for a specific candidate, voters must enter
that candidate’s official identification number into an elec-
tronic voting machine on election day.1 As a result, candi-
dates place their unique candidate identification numbers,
which are distributed by party elites, on everything from
pamphlets and stickers to clothing and cars. In congressio-
nal elections, candidate identification numbers have four
digits. These identification numbers may have repeating
digits, have adjacent consecutive integers or some combi-
nation of the two. Easily memorable identification numbers
are in short supply and highly coveted (Wylie, 2018).
Party leaders provide some congressional candidates
with suspiciously memorable identification numbers. For
example, in Sa˜o Paulo, Francisco Oliveira Silva, who is
better known as the clown Tiririca (Grumpy) had the iden-
tification number 2222. Similarly, in the state of Bahia,
F´elix Mendonc¸a Jr., the son of a popular mayor, held the
highly desirable identification number 1234. Bueno and
Dunning (2017) show that candidates provided identifica-
tion numbers with repeating digits and adjacent consecu-
tive integers are more likely to win office. It is unclear
though if party elites provide men “better numbers” than
women.
Campaign funds
Party officials also have discretion over the distribution of
financial resources. Candidates routinely use party funds to
produce campaign advertisements, pay campaign staff, and
to stage campaign events. Due to the large number of com-
petitors, candidates spend substantial sums of money to
distinguish themselves from their competitors (Avelino and
Fisch, 2018; Samuels, 2001a, 2001b). While some congres-
sional candidates have won office without spending lav-
ishly, campaign funds are universally regarded as an
important determinant of electoral success in Brazilian
congressional elections.
Businesses and private individuals have historically
been the principal source of campaign resources (Samuels,
2001a). Financial disclosures, however, show that political
parties are an increasingly important source of funds.2 In
2014, political parties donated more to congressional cam-
paigns than either corporations or private citizens (Silva
and Cervi, 2017). Party organizations provided congressio-
nal candidates cash or physical materials valued at R$450
million, about 150 million US dollars.
The criteria that underlie the distribution of parties’
financial resources is unclear. Even politicians themselves
report that they do not know how their respective parties
decide how to allocate funds (Ara´ujo and Borges, 2013).
Theory suggests that party elites will distribute financial
resources strategically to maximize the number of seats
they win and therefore privilege experienced politicians.
Extant work, though, shows that there is a relationship
between candidate gender and resource allocation deci-
sions (Sacchet, 2011; Sacchet and Speck, 2012; Wylie,
2018). Sacchet (2011) finds that in the 2006 and 2010
congressional elections, political parties distributed cam-
paign funds disproportionately to male candidates.
In 2010, Brazilian political parties were required to
spend 5% of party funds promoting women’s participation.
Political parties that year often spent little beyond this
amount (Wylie, 2018). Wylie et al. (2019) suggest that
women get less money because party elites only nominate
them to comply with the gender quota. In some instances,
the women know that they are “laranja” (orange) candi-
dates, individuals who do not have a chance of winning
and do not even campaign. Other women, however, run
earnest campaigns but do not receive party funds.
Broadcast airtime
Like candidate identification numbers, broadcast airtime is
a valuable campaign resource that party elites exclusively
control. Survey results indicate that voters regularly watch
the HGPE and suggests that advertising affects voter beha-
vior (Porto, 2006). Research on the relationship between
HGPE airtime and electoral performance are consistent
with this conclusion. Schmitt et al. (1999) as well as Speck
and Cervi (2016) find a positive relationship between tele-
vision airtime and vote share.3 Moreover, in a study that
addresses endogeneity and omitted variable bias threats,
Silveira and Mello (2011) show that television advertising
has a large causal effect on election outcomes. Given the
electoral value of HGPE airtime, it is not surprising that
political candidates covet it. Many congressional candi-
dates, however, never receive it.
The authors of Brazil’s 1988 Constitution established
the Hor´ario Gratuito de Propaganda Eleitoral (HGPE), a
publicly financed electoral program, to ensure that voters
receive information about the qualifications and policy
views of candidates from across the political spectrum
(Porto, 2006). This program grants political parties the
opportunity to broadcast campaign ads on television and
radio in the 45 days leading up to elections, but otherwise
bars the purchase of airtime for political purposes.4
Although free advertisement time given by the Brazilian
government has served as a democratizing force between
parties, it does not level the playing field between candi-
dates. HGPE airtime is distributed at the discretion of party
leaders.
Extant research indicates that party elites establish
“informal party lists” through the distribution of HGPE
airtime (Albuquerque et al., 2008; Schmitt et al., 1999).
Studies show that party leaders privilege incumbents over
nonincumbents (Janusz and Campos, forthcoming). More-
over, they reveal that women receive less airtime than men
(Ara´ujo and Borges, 2013; Carvalho et al., 2018; Romero

In the next section, we examine how party elites distri-
bute candidate identification numbers, financial resources,
and broadcast airtime. Theory suggests that party elites
distribute campaign resources efficiently to maximize the
number of seats the party wins. Our quantitative analysis
reveals that experienced politicians are privileged, but also
reveals that female candidates are marginalized.
Analysis of party behavior
To determine how candidate attributes affect access to
campaign resources, we draw on data from Brazil’s 2014
congressional elections. In the 2014 elections, 5,864 can-
didates competed for a seat in Brazil’s Chamber of Depu-
ties. Of those candidates, 29.4% identified themselves as
female. The women that competed in 2014 differed from
male candidates in observable and theoretically important
ways. Among the most important differences between men
and women is their levels of electoral experience. Female
candidates are significantly less likely than male candidates
to be incumbents or have held electoral office. Using OLS
regression we are able to control for theoretically important
attributes that may explain gender disparities in campaign
resources.
We utilize three different dependent variables in our
analysis of elite party behavior.5 In Model 1, our dependent
variable is the electoral quality of each candidate’s unique
identification number. This variable, which is coded in
accordance with the Bueno and Dunning (2017) “good
number” measure, reflects the number of repeating digits
and adjacent sequential integers.6 In Model 2, our depen-
dent variable is the value of campaign resources provided
by a candidate’s state political party. We measure this vari-
able in R$10,000 increments. Finally, in Model 3, our
dependent variable is the number of seconds of HGPE air-
time awarded to candidates.7 For this measure, we only
have data on the distribution of HGPE airtime in Rio de
Janeiro. Because state party elites exercise control over the
distribution of candidate identification numbers, campaign
funds and HGPE airtime, we employ state party fixed
effects.
In each model, we include a series of dummy variables
to capture a candidates’ level of electoral experience. The
first of these, the Incumbent Politician variable, takes the
value “1” if a candidate is running for reelection and “0” if
they are not. The second variable, Electoral Office Holder,
takes the value “1” if the candidate was elected to any
political office in the prior 10 years and “0” otherwise.8
Finally, the Prior Candidacy variable captures whether an
individual previously ran for elected office but was never
elected. This variable takes the value “1” if a candidate ran
for office in the past 10 years but never was elected and “0”
otherwise. We expect both the Incumbent Politician and the
Electoral Experience variables to produce positive, statis-
tically significant coefficients. The Prior Candidacy vari-
able could have a negative, positive or null effect on the
distribution of resources. The fact that a candidate has run
for office and lost likely sends a noisy signal to party elites
about that individual’s electoral prospects.
Besides electoral experience, we expect that women are
likely to be overlooked in the distribution of campaign
resources. We therefore include the Female Candidate
variable, which takes the value “1” if a politician identified
themselves as a woman when they registered to run for
public office and “0” otherwise. We expect this variable
to produce a negative, statistically significant coefficient in
each model.
Research indicates that candidate attributes besides
electoral experience and gender may affect their access to
resources. Therefore, we include measures of a candidate’s
level of education, occupation, age, and racial group mem-
bership as control variables (Cervi, 2011; Janusz and Cam-
pos, forthcoming; Romero et al., 2012). We include the
College Degree variable, which is coded “1” if a candidate
reported they have a college degree and “0” otherwise. We
expect candidates who have graduated college to receive
greater resources than candidates with less formal educa-
tion. The Businessperson variable is coded “1” if a person
identifies themselves a businessperson when they register
to run for office.9 Since research suggests that party elites
prefer to recruit business professionals, it is possible they
also privilege them when distributing resources. The Can-
didate Age variable is a nominal measure of a candidate’s
age at the time of registration. Older candidates may be
perceived as more mature and capable, and therefore pri-
vileged. Finally, we include a dichotomous measure of
candidate race. The Nonwhite Candidate variable is coded
“1” if a candidate did not identify themselves as a white
when they registered to run for office and “0” otherwise.
We expect party leaders will favor white candidates over
nonwhite candidates when allocating resources.

strong evidence that party elites distribute campaign

The results also reveal that party elites provide female can-

In Model 2, we explore the distribution of state party
campaign funds. Again, we find that incumbents and those
who have held elected office are the largest beneficiaries of
party support. Incumbents receive approximately
R$236,660 more than first-time candidates. Party elites

when distributing funds. While the amount prior office

receive, it is still sizable. Party elites provide candidates
that have held elected office just shy of R$25,000 more

Considering the importance of financial resources in con-
gressional elections and evidence that women face hurdles
to raising private funds (Ara´ujo and Borges, 2013; dos
Santos and Wylie, 2018; Wylie, 2020), the results of Model
2 suggest that party elites are not committed to helping
women win elected office.
In Model 3, we turn to the distribution of HGPE air-
time. In 2014, political parties in Rio de Janeiro distrib-
uted 422 minutes of broadcast airtime to congressional
candidates. We find that party leaders did not distribute
airtime equally. Incumbents and prior office holders
receive significantly more HGPE airtime than political
novices. In contrast to prior studies, which suggest that
women receive less HGPE airtime because they are scar-
cer among incumbents, we find that electoral experience
does not explain gender gaps in HGPE airtime. All else
equal, female nonincumbents receive 9.3 seconds less
than their male, nonincumbents copartisans. Considering
the size of Brazil’s congressional districts and voters’
reliance on television for political information, women
are thus at a considerable disadvantage.

experience and gender, in each model we also control for
educational attainment, occupation, age, and racial group
membership. Two findings warrant mention. First, we find
that the coefficient on the College Degree variable is pos-

candidates with college degrees receive better identifica-
tion numbers, more resources, and more HGPE airtime than
their less educated copartisans. Party bosses likely believe
that candidates with a college degree possess the skills and
knowledge necessary to run a competitive campaign (Cam-

of statistical significance in the second model, which
only uses data on congressional elections in Rio de
Janeiro, it continues to have the expected positive sign.
Finally, in Model 2 the HGPE Airtime variable has a
positive and statistically significant coefficient. This
indicates that the more airtime candidates receive, the
more votes they receive. Together, these results suggest
that party resources matter. Candidates that receive party
campaign resources ultimately attain more votes than
candidates who do not.
In additional to showing that campaign resources affect
electoral performance, Table 2 also reveals that there is a
relationship between gender and vote share. The negative,
statistically significant coefficient on the Female Candi-
date variable indicates that women in comparison to men
receive significantly fewer votes. This finding indicates
that resources disparities are not the only factor hindering
women from winning public office and points to the pos-
sibility that voters discriminate against women. While this
finding conflicts with the established literature, it is con-
sistent with the results of a recent voting experiment.
Batista Pereira (2020) finds that Brazilian voters are less
likely to vote for women when they can substitute ideolo-
gically close female candidates with male copartisans.
Discussion and conclusion
Political parties are central to electoral competition. Party
elites determine the types of candidates that run through
recruitment and nomination procedures. Moreover, they
influence which types of candidates win via the distribution
of resources. In this paper, we argue that even when polit-
ical parties nominate women, they may hinder them from
winning public office by withholding critical campaign
resources. We test this hypothesis in Brazil, where parties
regularly nominate women to run for office, but women are
not elected in proportion to the rates at which they run.
Using original data on three distinct types of campaign
resources, we show that Brazilian party elites overwhel-
mingly favor men over women. We find that party elites
provide women less advantageous candidate identification
numbers than men. Importantly, the distribution of candi-
date numbers is not unique to Brazil. In Colombia and Peru,
voters also cast their ballot for candidates by checking a
number next to a party label or by writing in a candidate
number. It is unclear, however, if party elites similarly
marginalize women. More broadly, our findings comple-
ment work on placement mandates (Corrˆea and Chaves,
2020; Jankowski and Marcinkiewicz, 2019; Schmidt,
2009). In countries with open-list systems, where political
parties rank candidates but voters can disturb the list,
receiving a higher rank increases the likelihood of electoral
success (Mustillo and Polga-Hecimovich, 2020). Since
party elites regularly rank women lower than men, it
reduces their likelihood of winning office (Corrˆea and
Chaves, 2020).
We also show that party elites provide women less cam-
paign resources than men. In Brazil, party elites have dis-
cretion over the distribution of campaign funds as well as
broadcast advertising time. While prior studies document
gender gaps in resources, they suggest disparities are due to
differences in electoral experience. In contrast, we find that
gender disparities in party support are not attributable to
differences in candidate quality. This finding suggests that
party leaders discriminate against women when allocating

campaign resources and has critical implications for cam-
paign finance reforms.
In 2015, corporations were barred from donating to cam-
paigns and an electoral fund was established to underwrite
campaigns. According to a judicial ruling, 30% of those
funds are supposed to go to women. Wylie (2020) shows
that the change led more women to run and that the gender
gap in campaign revenues diminished. Our findings suggest
that gender gaps in resources likely persisted because of
party elites, the individuals empowered by the change, con-
tinued to favor men. We find some evidence of this. In
2018, party elites provided female candidates R$2,900 less
than male candidates. In other work, Wylie (2018) shows
that female party leaders are more likely to nominate
women candidates. Future studies should explore if women
are also more likely to fund female candidates. Our study
shows that party gatekeeping does not end at the nomina-
tion stage. When parties can nominate large numbers of
candidates and elites exclusively control the distribution
of critical campaign resources, resource gatekeeping may
occur.
Unlike money, which candidates can theoretically attain
from individual donors, airtime is exclusively distributed
by party elites. In Europe and Latin America, other coun-
tries have similar systems. Moreover, the Brazilian model
has been replicated across the region (Casas-Zamora,
2016). The extent to which party elites in those countries
privilege men over women when distributing television
time however has received scant scholarly attention. The
Brazilian case demonstrates that public advertising may
level the playing field between parties, but it will not neces-
sarily do so within parties. If parties were compelled to
distribute airtime equally as well as report how they allo-
cate it, gender gaps in media advertising could be reduced
and women’s representation would likely increase.
To push the literature on political parties and gender
equality forward, a first-order priority should be to explore
the extent parties have formal criteria for distributing
resources and whether candidates are aware of them. To
date, research suggests that resource allocation decisions
are made in smoke-filled rooms and that candidates are
ignorant of selection criteria (Ara´ujo and Borges, 2013).
Future studies should survey party elites to determine if
they are prejudiced against women or believe voters prefer
men. Moreover, our findings suggest that women are not
the only type of candidates that party elites may hinder
from winning office. Party elites may also hinder ethnic,
racial, and religious minorities from winning office by
withholding resources. While several studies explore this
possibility in developed democracies (Dancygier et al.,
2020; Fraga and Hassell, 2018; Hassell and Visalvanich,
2019), it has received comparatively little attention in
developing democracies.
It is also important to investigate how the distribution of
resources affects candidate behavior. For instance, do
candidates that receive limited party assistance give up
on campaigning before election day? Moreover, scholars
should explore whether candidates who receive scant party
support choose to participate in future elections, and if so,
whether they switch political parties? These questions
could be studied in the Brazilian context, but they are
applicable to all democracies.
